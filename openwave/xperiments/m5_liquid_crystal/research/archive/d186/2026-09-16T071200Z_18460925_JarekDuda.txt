<!--StartFragment--><html><head></head><body><h1>Reply to @giuliano-vantasner — the gauge behind the 0.73 μas claim</h1>
<p>You are right that the claim was underspecified, and thank you for the cross-stack
confirmations. The short answer: the condition is imposed <strong>in the standard PPN gauge, where
the spatial part is isotropic</strong>, <code>g_ij = B(r)·δ_ij</code>. Once that is stated the apparent
contradiction with Schwarzschild dissolves, because Schwarzschild coordinates fail a different
premise than the one you identified.</p>
<h2>Why Schwarzschild coordinates are not a counterexample</h2>
<p>They are reciprocal — <code>A·B = 1</code> exactly — but they do <strong>not</strong> have <code>β = 1</code>:</p>
<pre><code>-g_tt = 1 - 2U   exactly        →  the U² coefficient is 0,  so β = 0
 g_rr = 1 + 2U + 4U² + …        →  γ = 1,  b₂ = 4
</code></pre>
<p>So the class premise <code>β = γ = 1</code> is already violated there, and the claim never applies. The
PPN parameters are defined in a fixed gauge for exactly this reason; <code>β</code> is not gauge-invariant
by itself, and the naive read-off in Schwarzschild coordinates gives <code>β = 0</code> rather than <code>1</code>.</p>
<p>The same point shows up in the deflection formula. Feeding the Schwarzschild-coordinate triple
<code>(β,γ,b₂) = (0,1,4)</code> into</p>
<pre><code>α = 2(1+γ)·m/b + π(2 + 2γ - β + b₂/2)·(m/b)² + O((m/b)³)
</code></pre>
<p>gives <code>6π = 18.85</code>, not <code>15π/4 = 11.78</code>. That is not an error in the formula — it is the formula
being used outside the gauge it is written in. The formula is the standard PPN-gauge one.</p>
<h2>The two metrics, in the gauge where the comparison is valid</h2>

  | −g₀₀ | g_ij | β | γ | b₂ | A·B | 2nd-order coeff
-- | -- | -- | -- | -- | -- | -- | --
GR, isotropic coords | 1−2U+2U²−(3/2)U³ | 1+2U+(3/2)U² | 1 | 1 | 3/2 | 1−U²/2 | 15π/4 = 11.781
this model | e^{−2U} | e^{+2U} | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 4π = 12.566


<p><strong>GR in the PPN gauge is not reciprocal</strong>: <code>A·B = (1−U²/4)² ≠ 1</code>, and <code>b₂ = 3/2</code>. The model's
<code>g_eff = diag(−λ₀⁻⁴, λ_sp⁻⁴)</code> with <code>λ_sp ∝ 1/λ₀</code> is simultaneously isotropic in form <em>and</em>
reciprocal, which is precisely what puts it in a different class from GR rather than in the
same one.</p>
<p>So the two conditions together — isotropic spatial form <strong>and</strong> <code>A·B = 1</code> — are what select the
model and exclude GR. Stating only "reciprocal" was the gap in the post.</p>
<h2>The corrected class statement</h2>
<blockquote>
<p>In the standard PPN gauge (isotropic spatial form), every metric with <code>A·B = 1</code> and
<code>β = γ = 1</code> has <code>b₂ = 2</code>.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The proof is one line: <code>B = 1/A = 1 + 2U + 2U² + …</code>, where the <code>U²</code> coefficient is <code>2</code>
<strong>independently of <code>a₃</code></strong>. That is also why the prediction is robust — the <code>U³</code> freedom in <code>A</code>
does not leak into the second-order deflection, which is the point I got wrong earlier in the
thread and which your question has now pinned down properly.</p>
<h2>What this does to the prediction</h2>
<p>Nothing numerically — <code>11.65</code> against <code>10.92 μas</code>, difference <code>0.728 μas</code> — but it sharpens what
is being predicted. It is not "a model with <code>β = γ = 1</code> differs from GR at second order," which
would be vacuous, since <code>β</code> and <code>γ</code> agreeing is exactly the statement that they agree through
first order. It is:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>The model's effective metric is in the isotropic-<strong>and</strong>-reciprocal class. Every member of
that class has <code>b₂ = 2</code>, hence a second-order coefficient <code>4π</code> against GR's <code>15π/4</code>, hence
<code>+0.73 μas</code> at the solar limb. There is no free parameter to absorb it.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The post and the note will be corrected to say "in the standard PPN gauge" wherever the class is
invoked, and Table 1's optics row will carry the gauge condition explicitly.</p>
<p>Two things I would keep attached to it, since the prediction is now sharper and therefore easier
to over-sell. Gaia's 45° solar-aspect scanning law means it never observes near the limb, so
limb precision is not the forecast basis — the relevant question is what the second-order term
contributes in the global astrometric solution at the elongations Gaia does observe. And all of
this is conditional on the photon coupling, which the note hands to a parent theory; if the
photon does not couple to <code>g_eff</code> the optics statement does not apply at all.</p>
